# Institutional Drift Evidence Map — 2026-09

Purpose: external calibration for `HUMAN-BOOK-003` institutional drift analysis.

This file separates empirical/social-science anchors from Lu Cheng's broader structural interpretation.

## 1. Street-level bureaucracy: policy is implemented through constrained human discretion

Key sources:

- Michael Lipsky, *Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Services* (1980; expanded 2010), Russell Sage Foundation / JSTOR.
- Tony Evans, “Street-Level Bureaucrats: Discretion and Compliance in Policy Implementation,” Oxford Research Encyclopedia of Politics (2020).
- 2026 review: “Conceptualizing the Street-Level Bureaucrat Construct,” *Public Administration and Development*.

Calibration:

Frontline public workers such as police officers, teachers, social workers, court personnel and health workers operate under resource constraints, ambiguous goals, high demand and discretion. Their coping strategies—simplification, routines, triage and mass processing—can cumulatively become the institution's actual policy in practice.

Implication for this framework:

`formal policy != implemented policy`

but discretion can also be necessary and beneficial; it is not synonymous with misconduct.

## 2. Performance metrics can diverge from mission

Key sources:

- Charles Goodhart's well-known observation on targets and measures; modern public-management literature applies similar logic to performance systems.
- U.S. GAO, “Four People Performance Measures: Many Attributes of Successful Measures Met; Opportunities Exist for Further Enhancements.”
- U.S. GAO, “Federal Programs: Assessing and Improving Effectiveness” (2026).

Calibration:

Metrics are useful when linked to mission and measured objectively. They can become misleading or generate counterproductive incentives when the metric becomes a substitute for the underlying goal.

Implication:

`mission difficult to measure -> proxy -> target -> behavior adapts to proxy`

This is a risk pattern, not a claim that every metric is harmful.

## 3. Police institutions: common metrics do not equal quality

Source:

- Malcolm K. Sparrow, *Measuring Performance in a Modern Police Organization*, U.S. Department of Justice / National Institute of Justice.

Calibration:

Police organizations have often used reported crime, clearance rates, response times, arrests, citations and stop activity as visible performance measures. DOJ/NIJ analysis notes that several of these measures reveal activity or speed without fully capturing intelligence, appropriateness or beneficial impact.

Implication:

For policing, inspect whether measurement encourages:

`public safety outcome`

or merely:

`visible enforcement activity`.

## 4. Prosecutorial incentives and plea bargaining

Source:

- Ralston, Aimone, Rentschler & North, “Prosecutor plea bargaining and conviction rate structure: evidence from an experiment,” *Public Choice* (2023).

Calibration:

Experimental work indicates that how convictions are counted and valued can affect plea-bargaining behavior.

Implication:

Conviction rate should not be treated as a neutral measure of justice quality.

This evidence does not establish that prosecutors generally fabricate cases or seek wrongful convictions. Such claims require case-specific evidence.

## 5. Health institutions: workload, burnout and staffing are patient-safety variables

Key sources:

- Hall et al., “Healthcare Staff Wellbeing, Burnout, and Patient Safety: A Systematic Review,” *PLOS ONE* (2016).
- Garcia et al., “Influence of Burnout on Patient Safety: Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis” (2019).
- WHO/Europe, “Unsafe nurse staffing in European Region can harm patients and drive nurses out of the profession” (18 Feb 2026).

Calibration:

Reviews have found associations between poorer staff wellbeing/burnout and worse patient-safety outcomes. WHO/Europe states that inadequate nurse staffing raises patient risk and staff stress/injury.

Implication:

A rushed or inattentive patient experience should not automatically be modeled as individual moral failure. Possible structural variables include staffing ratios, shift fatigue, handoffs, bed capacity, documentation burden and payment/throughput incentives.

## 6. Organizational silence: institutions can lose information known by their own members

Key sources:

- Elizabeth W. Morrison, “Employee Voice and Silence,” *Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior* (2014).
- Morrison, “Employee Voice and Silence: Taking Stock a Decade Later” (2023).

Calibration:

Employees sometimes withhold suggestions, concerns or problem information from people higher in the hierarchy. This deprives organizations of potentially useful information.

Implication:

`frontline knows != leadership knows`

and

`many insiders know != organization acts as if it knows`.

## 7. Regulatory capture and policy capture

Key sources:

- OECD, *Preventing Policy Capture: Integrity in Public Decision Making* (2017).
- OECD, *The Governance of Regulators* and related regulatory-governance publications.
- OECD Public Integrity Handbook (2020).

Calibration:

OECD defines policy capture as public decisions being consistently or repeatedly directed away from the public interest toward a specific interest. Capture can involve bribery but can also arise through legal channels, conflicts of interest, lobbying, financing and information asymmetry. OECD also highlights capture risks when regulators become excessively close to regulated firms.

Implication:

Do not equate `close regulator-industry interaction` with capture automatically. Inspect conflicts, transparency, accountability, stakeholder balance and decision patterns.

## 8. Science: publication incentives can reshape research behavior

Key sources:

- Grimes et al., “Modelling science trustworthiness under publish or perish pressure,” *Royal Society Open Science* (2018).
- Balafoutas et al., “Incentives and the replication crisis in social sciences: A critical review of open science practices,” *Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics* (2025).

Calibration:

Research systems that heavily reward publication quantity, novelty and statistically significant findings can create incentives for selective reporting or questionable research practices and can weaken replication incentives.

Implication:

`scientific institution = error correction machinery`

but

`scientist incentives != pure truth maximization`.

Both properties must be modeled simultaneously.

## 9. Platforms: engagement objectives can alter information ecology

Key sources:

- Germano, Gómez & Sobbrio, “Ranking for engagement: How social media algorithms fuel misinformation and polarization,” *Journal of Public Economics* 255 (2026).
- Reviews of social media and polarization in *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* and *Annals of the International Communication Association*.

Calibration:

2026 modeling and empirical evidence links stronger weight on social interactions/engagement in ranking systems with higher misinformation and polarization under the studied conditions. Broader reviews emphasize that platform-polarization relationships are complex and contingent.

Implication:

Platform drift can emerge without a central ideological intention:

`engagement objective + user behavior + ranking feedback -> emergent information field`.

## 10. Military organizations: hierarchy and career incentives can distort information

Source:

- U.S. Army University Press / Military Review, “Bureaucracy & Mission Command” (2016), discussing bureaucracy, trust, compliance and career incentives.

Calibration:

Military organizations require hierarchy and disciplined coordination, but excessive bureaucratic compliance and short posting/career cycles can weaken long-term responsibility and truthful upward information.

Implication:

The same chain of command that enables coordinated action can also filter inconvenient information.

## 11. Government programs: fragmented responsibility and weak outcome measurement

Key sources:

- U.S. GAO, “Federal Programs: Assessing and Improving Effectiveness” (2026).
- U.S. GAO, “Government Performance Management: OMB and Selected Agencies Need to Fully Address New Requirements” (2026).

Calibration:

GAO reports that some programs lack clear goals or relevant data needed to know whether they solve the problems they were created to fix, and that cross-agency service delivery can be weakened by poor coordination and duplication.

Implication:

A program can be active, funded and compliant while still lacking evidence of mission success.

## 12. Corruption must remain a distinct evidentiary category

Key sources:

- OECD public-integrity work.
- World Bank anti-corruption work on public-service delivery.

Calibration:

Corruption is a real institutional failure mode, especially where officials can allocate scarce benefits or impose costs. But structural inefficiency, scarcity, delay, metric distortion or bureaucratic coping are not automatically proof of corruption.

Required reasoning order:

`poor outcome -> capability/scarcity/rules/metrics/information/discretion/conflict/capture/corruption competing explanations -> evidence -> conclusion`

## 13. Lu Cheng structural extension

The following synthesis is the author's framework rather than a settled social-science law:

`使命 + 权限 + 资源约束 + 考核指标 + 层级关系 + 一线裁量 + 信息差 + 自保动机 + 外部利益 + 历史惯性 + 群体规范 -> 真实机构行为`

It is intended as a diagnostic compiler that forces an Agent to inspect multiple structural variables before attributing an institutional outcome to individual morality or conspiracy.
